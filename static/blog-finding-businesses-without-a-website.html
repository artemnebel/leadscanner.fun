How to Find Businesses Without a Website in 2026

Every web designer's biggest bottleneck isn't skill, it's finding people to pitch. You can build a beautiful site in a weekend, but if you spend three hours a day scrolling Google Maps looking for businesses that might not have a website, you're burning your most valuable resource on the least valuable part of the job.

Here's how the search for "businesses without a website" actually works, from the manual version most people start with to the point where it makes sense to automate it.

The manual method, and why it breaks down

The classic approach is: open Google Maps, search a category like "plumbers" or "hair salons" in a city, and click through each result one at a time. You're looking for two things: a business that has a phone number and reviews (so you know it's real and active), and no website link on its profile.

This works. The problem is volume. A single mid-size city might return 60-100 results for one category. Clicking into each listing, checking for a website field, and writing down the ones that qualify takes real time, and you have to repeat it for every category and every city you want to work. Most people give up after a page or two and end up pitching whoever's easiest to find rather than whoever's the best fit.

What actually makes a lead "qualified"

Before you even think about tooling, it helps to be specific about what you're looking for. A good lead for cold web design outreach usually has:

  • No website, obviously, but specifically no website field on their Google Business Profile — a Facebook page or Instagram alone doesn't count against them, since plenty of good prospects rely on social only.
  • An active phone number. If you can't reach them, you can't pitch them.
  • Real reviews. A business with 20+ reviews and a 4-star average is clearly operating and getting customers; it's just not converting as many as it could online. That's a much stronger pitch than a business with zero reviews that might not even be open anymore.
  • A category that benefits from a site. Restaurants, contractors, salons, and local service businesses convert well because customers actively search for them online before calling. Something like a storage unit facility, less so.

Filtering for these up front saves you from wasting outreach on businesses that were never going to say yes.

Widening the net without widening the effort

Once you've worked through the obvious categories in your own city, the next move is usually one of two things: expand the radius, or expand the category list. Both multiply the number of listings you'd need to manually check, which is exactly where doing this by hand stops being worth your time.

This is the actual reason automated scanning tools exist. A tool that queries the Google Places API directly can search a category across an entire radius, check every result's website field programmatically, and hand you only the businesses that qualify, in seconds instead of hours. Lead Scanner does exactly this: pick a category, drag a radius on the map, and it returns every matching business with no website, along with their phone number, rating, and review count, ready to export.

A simple workflow that scales

  1. Pick a city and a handful of categories that convert well in your experience (contractors, restaurants, and personal-care businesses are a solid starting set).
  2. Scan each category and export the results with contact info and Maps links.
  3. Sort by review count and rating so you're pitching active, credible businesses first.
  4. Move to the next city or radius once you've worked through the list, rather than re-searching the same area repeatedly.

The businesses themselves haven't gotten harder to find. What's changed is that there's no reason left to find them one click at a time.
